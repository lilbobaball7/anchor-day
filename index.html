    Why this planner is built this way

    Every part of this is here for an ADHD-specific reason. You don't need to read this to use it.

    It shows the time, loudly

    ADHD comes with "time blindness" — time feels like now or not-now. The live clock, the Right Now card and the progress bar make time something you can see instead of having to track in your head.

    Only three things

    Long to-do lists cause overwhelm and shutdown. Choosing a small number of priorities lowers the barrier to starting and makes "done" reachable.

    Anchors, not a rigid timetable

    A few fixed points (⚓) — wake, meals, wind-down — hold the day together. Everything between them can flex. Habits are easiest to keep when stacked onto things you already do.

    The focus sprint

    Starting is the hardest part. A short, visible timer turns a vague task into "just do it until the clock stops," which is far easier to begin. Doing it alongside someone ("body doubling") helps even more.

    The brain dump

    Working memory is leaky. Parking a stray thought on paper (or here) stops it from hijacking what you're doing, without losing it.

    Wins, not guilt

    A missed block isn't failure and nothing turns angry-red. The win counter and the "jump back in" button are built to break the shame spiral — the thing that actually derails ADHD days — and get you re-started gently.
